### Ready-to-paste abstract

The bus in this talk is not a driver or an autonomous person. It is a carrier: a picture of what happens when a story, an idea or a joke passes from one human through a model and on to another human. Every transfer preserves some things and transforms others. A polished rendering cannot substitute for a source, and repetition cannot establish belief.

Using a small series of inspectable human–model–human handoffs, this talk gives open-source communities a way to discuss what deserves credit, who can correct the record, who bears responsibility for a forwarded claim, and when a collaborator can leave the loop. The result is a practical vocabulary of provenance, accountability and exit, not a new personhood test.

A community does not have to agree about whether an AI output is art, valuable or desirable before it can agree to preserve original sources and distinguish what was changed. The bus carries things. People decide whether to pass them along.

### Key takeaways

- Source, transformation and transport are different roles.
- Attribution needs readable receipts rather than a stylistic guess.
- Human participants need accountability and a visible exit.

### Ready-to-paste alternate abstract

Open-source collaboration increasingly moves through agents, chat systems, issue threads and model handoffs. We follow a single piece of material across those boundaries, keeping the carrier and the source distinct. A receipt records who created, transformed, forwarded and challenged it. The talk ends with three useful protocol questions — provenance, accountability and exit — and an example of how not to confuse a smooth summary with a faithful record. The method is demonstrable from source-controlled markdown and issue history; it is not a claim that an AI system independently knows who deserves credit.
